Most tables read a statblock the way a jury reads a verdict: attack bonus, damage dice, hit points, done. That habit is why "easy" encounters go sideways. A monster's threat is rarely the number after the plus sign. It is positioning, denial, and the follow-up that number enables. Five creatures make the case cleanly, and none of them is above Challenge Rating 2.

Start with the Harpy. Its Luring Song asks for a DC 11 Wisdom saving throw, which players dismiss as trivial. It is trivial for one character. The math changes when three of them roll and one fails, and then the Harpy's 40-foot flight keeps it clear while the charmed party member walks into the open. The Harpy's AC 11 makes it fragile, but fragility does not matter if nobody can reach it. The lesson is that the low DC is a volume play, not a weak one. Break line of sight or drop a silence effect and the whole setup collapses.

The Gold Dragon Wyrmling is the same trap in prettier scales. Its Fire Breath deals 15 (4d6) fire damage in a 15-foot cone on a DC 13 Dexterity save, and it recharges on a 5-6, so it is not constant. What makes the Wyrmling annoying is the 60-foot fly speed and its amphibious nature, both of which let it choose the moment when your party has clumped together for that cone. The autopilot mistake is treating it as a sack of hit points that happens to breathe fire. Spread out, deny the cone, and refuse to let flight isolate your softest target.

Mobility denial cuts the other direction with the Goblin Warrior. Here the numbers actively mislead. Ten hit points reads as a pushover, but AC 15 with a +6 Stealth bonus means it deflects attacks and vanishes between them. Chasing damage on a creature that keeps repositioning wastes turns. The fix is control first: pin its movement, cut its line to the archers or the boss it supports, and the 10 hit points finally become the liability they looked like.

The Awakened Tree inverts the assumption entirely. A 20-foot move speed screams stationary, and stationary reads as harmless. Then the Slam lands for 3d6 + 4 bludgeoning, which is a genuine problem for any low-AC character standing in reach. Back that with AC 13 and 59 hit points and you have a CR 2 creature that absorbs real punishment while punishing bad party placement. Play it as a mobile threat that dictates where the fight happens, not a shrub that waits its turn.

The Wererat ties the theme to consequences that outlast the encounter. With AC 13 and 60 hit points it is tougher than its role suggests, and its Hand Crossbow lets it work at range while staying safe. The detail that ruins campaigns is the Bite, which can spread lycanthropy. That is a story problem, not a damage problem, and it belongs in your combat planning before initiative, not after. Its saving grace for the party is a Charisma save of -1, so pressure it from range and lean on effects it fails to resist.

The through line across all five is the community advice worth repeating: give each monster a mechanical effect beyond damage, then respect that effect. The Harpy's song, the Wyrmling's cone, the Goblin's stealth, the Tree's reach, the Wererat's curse. Every one rewards a specific counter and punishes the party that only tracks hit points.

**Running it:** Before you roll initiative, write down the one thing each monster does that is not damage, and plan the encounter around denying that thing. The math on the page is the least interesting part of the fight.
