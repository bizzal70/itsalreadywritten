Most tables read a spell and immediately look for the damage line. If there is no damage line, the spell gets slotted as "situational" and forgotten. This is the single most common way to waste a spell. The use case, meaning the specific problem a spell is built to solve, matters more than its raw numbers. A spell with no attack roll can decide a fight faster than one that deals a dozen points, provided you understand what it actually does to the board. Five spells illustrate this better than any lecture.

Start with Restoration, range Close, duration Instant. It removes a single curse, illness, or affliction from a target creature. Players burn it to patch hit points, which it does not even do. The correct read is narrower and far stronger: Restoration ends the condition that is blocking the party from progressing or surviving at all. The curse that halves a fighter's attacks, the illness ticking toward death. Restoration ends it, instantly, no roll required. That is a problem solved, not a number nudged.

Cacklerot works the same way from a different angle. Range Close, duration Focus. It renders a creature of level 4 or lower helpless with laughter for as long as you keep concentrating, with no saving throw offered. The misuse is spraying it at whatever is closest. The value is surgical: pick the one creature whose removal actually shifts the tactical balance, the caster behind the wall or the brute anchoring the line, and take it out of the fight entirely while you hold focus. One good target beats three convenient ones.

Illusion extends the principle past combat. Range Far, duration Focus, creating a visible and audible deception within a near-sized cube. It deals no damage, which is exactly why people underuse it. Creatures convinced the illusion is real act as though it is real. Most players reach for it as a battlefield distraction and stop there. The larger use is information and access: a convincing illusion can open a door, extract a confession, or move an enemy to ground of your choosing. That changes the stakes of a scene, not just the positioning inside it.

Freya's Omen is the clearest case of a spell punished by bad timing. It is a tier 4 Seer spell that prevents spell loss on a failed cast, but only for 1d6 rounds, and it does nothing against critical backfires. Cast it early "just in case" and the duration bleeds out before it matters. It is not a safety net you wear all day. It is a window you open the instant before you attempt the spell you cannot afford to lose, when a failed cast would otherwise cost you an action and a prepared slot you needed.

Willowman closes the set by breaking an assumption outright. Range Near, Instant, targeting creatures of level 2 or less and forcing a morale check. The overlooked part: it compels that check even on creatures you expect to be morale-immune, including undead. A skeleton that should fight to the last bone can be made to roll. Deploy Willowman precisely where the enemy's expected behavior is their strength, and turn that reliability against them.

The thread across all five is the same. Restoration removes the condition, Cacklerot removes the key target, Illusion removes the enemy's grip on reality, Freya's Omen removes the cost of a risky cast, and Willowman removes the certainty of an immune creature's resolve. None of them lead with damage. All of them decide outcomes.

**Running it:** Before you cast, finish this sentence out loud: "This spell changes the situation by removing ____." If you cannot name what it removes, you are using it wrong.
