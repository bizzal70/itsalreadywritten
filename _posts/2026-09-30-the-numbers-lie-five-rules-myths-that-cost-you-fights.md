The Rules Myth is the quiet tax on your table. It rarely announces itself with a wrong ruling. It shows up as a wasted spell slot, a slain prisoner you needed alive, or a rogue standing in the open because "there's a wall right there." Nobody read anything incorrectly. They read nothing at all, and filled the gap with a reasonable-sounding assumption. Five rules take the brunt of this, and they connect into a single lesson about what a mechanic actually promises versus what players hope it delivers.

Start with Healing, because it disappoints more often than any other rule. Cure Wounds and a Potion of Healing restore Hit Points and nothing else. Not conditions. Not exhaustion. When a poisoned ally goes down and the cleric burns a slot to bring them back up, that ally stands up still poisoned, and the slot did nothing about the actual problem. Healing addresses the meter, not the ailment. Conditions want Lesser Restoration, a different tool entirely. Confusing the two is the most expensive misread in the game because it happens under pressure.

That meter deserves attention on its own terms. Hit Points are not a fuel gauge for meat. They also track a creature's will to live, its resolve, its capacity to keep standing. This is not flavor trivia. Treating Hit Points as pure physical durability blinds you to the moment when the numbers are telling you to change tactics. Low Hit Points are a signal to disengage, negotiate, or reach for a non-combat exit while resources still exist. Players who read the total as "how much beating my body absorbs" fight to zero every time. Players who read it as resolve know when the fight is no longer the plan.

When you do drive an enemy to zero, remember Knocking out a Creature. Reducing a creature to 0 Hit Points with a melee attack lets you choose instead to leave it at 1 Hit Point and apply the Unconscious condition. This is a decision you make at the moment of the killing blow, not a mercy you negotiate afterward. It exists so you can take a prisoner for interrogation, spare a charmed innocent, or step around a moral complication without inventing house rules. The mechanic is already there. Most tables simply forget it is an option and default to a corpse.

Cover feeds the same trap from the defensive side. There are three degrees, and the middle ones matter most because they get mistaken for the extreme. Half cover grants +2 to AC and Dexterity saving throws. Three-quarters cover grants +5. Total cover blocks the attack or effect entirely. The recurring error is assuming any obstacle equals total cover, when a low wall or a barrel usually grants half. Cover rewards reading the line of sight honestly and claiming the exact degree the terrain provides, not the degree you wish it provided.

Skill Proficiencies close the set with the purest version of the myth. A Skill Proficiency adds your proficiency bonus to a specific ability check. That is the whole function. It modifies the d20 roll. It does not guarantee success, because the DC lives with the DM, not on your sheet. A +11 Persuasion still fails against a high enough DC and a low enough roll. Proficiency tells you which problems suit your character, not which problems solve themselves. It is a lever, not a bypass.

The through-line is simple. Every one of these rules does precisely what it states and refuses to do the generous thing you assumed. The myth is always the assumption, never the text.

**Running it:** Before you spend a resource, say out loud what the rule actually restores, modifies, or grants, and if you cannot state it in one sentence, you are about to pay the Rules Myth tax.
