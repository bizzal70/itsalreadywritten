Monster Tactic is the part of the book players never see and judges most often skip. A statline is not a creature. The creature is the statline plus the choices a judge makes with it: where it hides, when it moves, what it targets, and how its special effects compound over rounds. Read the entry the way its designer wrote it and you find that most DCC monsters already tell you how they want to be run. The oversight that gets characters killed is almost always a line in the text that nobody read closely. Five creatures make the case.

Start with the Lava Ooze Primeval Slime, because it looks like the least of the five and is not. Its camouflage grants +10 to stealth checks in volcanic terrain, and the common mistake is ignoring that bonus, which turns a slow blob into an ambush. Movement is only 5 feet, so once it lands a pseudopod hit for 1d4 plus burning touch, position is everything. Burning touch stacks if left alone. The counter is written into the entry: cold and water do double damage. Control its position, hit the vulnerability, and a feared ambusher becomes a crawling target.

The Plagued Tar Elemental runs the same pattern at higher pressure. Each hit, melee or ranged, lands 3d6 plus tar pox, and tar pox complicates recovery in a way players underestimate because they are watching the hit point total instead of the pressure cycle. With AC 19, landing clean hits takes dedicated effort, and parties waste rounds grinding raw damage while the pox accumulates. The entry hands you the same escape hatch as the slime: immunity to fire, vulnerability to cold and water for double damage. Exploit it early or lose the attrition war.

The Siege Colossus does not bother with subtlety. It carries 3d24 action dice, which means several attacks per round combining stomps and long-range fire. The stomp deals 2d24+10 and knocks a target prone unless it makes a DC 20 Reflex save, and the frequently ignored threat is the 500 foot range on its ballista and trebuchet, which chews up a party before anyone closes. The text points at terrain. Break line of sight, engage from multiple angles, and split its attention instead of standing in the open to absorb trebuchet fire.

Zamyra is the Colossus problem at melee range. Its 8d20+1d20 action dice feed a barrage of attacks and spells at +27 to hit, against a high AC, and walking into direct combat without a plan is how a party folds in a round. The entry's advice is movement and target priority: disrupt its positioning and limit the attacks it can bring to bear, rather than trading blows it will win.

The Rust Spider is the odd one out, and the most instructive for exactly that reason. Its damage is not the threat. Its rust glob corrodes metal armor and weapons within 30 feet, and a 40 foot move with a 20 foot climb speed lets it work vertical terrain and stay out of reach while it ruins your gear. Lose your weapon mid-fight and the math changes permanently. The counter is preparation: non-metal weapons, or killing the spider before it globs anyone.

The through line across all five is that the dangerous mechanic is rarely the one on the damage line. It is the camouflage bonus, the tar pox cycle, the trebuchet range, the action dice count, the rust glob. Each entry names its own weakness or its own counterplay in the same paragraph.

**Running it:** Before the fight, read the monster's entry out loud to yourself and underline the one sentence that is not about damage. That sentence is the fight.
