Most tables read monster stat blocks as a list of damage dice. The book asks for more. Monster Tactic is the practice of using a creature's abilities the way the creature would, which means understanding what a monster wants to do before it rolls initiative. A monster that spends its turn well can erase a party's plan, and the parts of the stat block players skim are usually the parts that do it. Below are five creatures whose lessons overlap.

Start with disruption of the party's own actions. The Wizard Viperian's Focus ability imposes disadvantage on all spellcasting checks for enemies within near range. This is not a damage source, it is a leash. A spell-reliant party that clusters near the Viperian is fighting at half strength on every cast, and many groups never notice why their casters keep failing. The counter is distance. Force the Viperian to chase you and it cannot keep Focus applied consistently, because it has to spend its movement instead of holding its ground.

The Archangel plays a similar game from the other direction. Its Crown of Fire raises the DC to 18 to cast any hostile spell in its presence, which turns every offensive cast into a gamble. Its Command talent forces a DC 18 Charisma save to resist, and players routinely underestimate how often that single ability rewrites their turn order. The mistake is casting into the Crown at the wrong moment and burning a resource for nothing. Handle the Archangel through positioning: pin its flight, isolate it from allies, and choose your casting windows rather than letting it dictate them.

The Sphinx is the hard lock. Its Roar replaces all of the Sphinx's attacks and demands a DC 18 Charisma save or paralysis for 1d4 rounds, reaching every creature within far range. A single failed save can remove a character from several rounds of the fight, and a bad round of saves can freeze the whole party. Time Bend compounds the problem, freezing a near target for 1d4 rounds on a failed DC 14 Wisdom save. Spread out so Roar cannot catch everyone at once, and treat those Wisdom saves as the priority against Time Bend.

The Dunefiend teaches the value of the clock. It can attack twice with its Tear at +4 for 1d8, but its real threat is the Howl, which reaches resting creatures at far range and forces a DC check that disrupts their rest. In a system where rest is how you recover resources, letting the Dunefiend live is letting your recovery get taxed. The lesson is simple: kill it before it can Howl, and treat it as a priority target whenever a rest matters.

The Giant Bat closes the set with the cheapest version of the same idea. It has AC 12 and 9 hit points, so it dies fast, but its near flying movement lets it reposition and bite for 1d6 at +2 before the party reacts. Ignore it and it picks the target it wants. The fix is to remove it early or use terrain to close off its lanes.

The thread running through all five is action economy and range. The Wizard Viperian and the Archangel tax your actions, the Sphinx and the Dunefiend delete or interrupt them, and the Giant Bat exploits the ones you waste on poor positioning. Read the ability that is not damage, because that is usually the one the monster wins with.

**Running it:** Before initiative, name the one non-damage ability on each stat block and decide what range denies it, then let the monster spend its turns chasing that range instead of chasing hit points.
