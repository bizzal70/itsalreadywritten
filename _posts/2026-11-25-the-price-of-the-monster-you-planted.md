An Encounter Seed is not a monster you drop on the table. It is a problem you hand the party with a price attached. The difference matters because most groups do not fear a stat block. They fear consequences they can see coming and cannot easily stop. A seed that costs nothing teaches players to swing until the hit points run out. A seed that costs something teaches them to think. Five creatures from the book make this point if you run them for what they actually do rather than for their silhouette.

Start with the Wyvern, which is routinely run for flavor and should be run for stakes. The rend attacks are the obvious threat and the reason tables underestimate it. The real danger is the stinger, which forces a DC 15 Constitution save or deals 2d10 on a failure. That is not chip damage. A party that ignores the poison and trades blows on the rends alone can turn a winnable skirmish into a body count. Seed it so that the poison is the lesson: reward high Constitution saves and mitigation, and punish the group that treats the tail as decoration.

The Sea Hag charges a different kind of price, and the price is timing. Terrify triggers when a creature first sees her true form, not when she attacks. Groups who misread that window waste actions bracing for the wrong moment. Shapechange is the tool that makes the timing hers. Have her approach unseen, close the distance, then reveal her true form at the start of combat so Terrify lands exactly when players most need their rolls to connect. Disadvantage imposed on the first round is a cost they cannot buy back.

The Rot Flower looks like the easiest kill at the table and that is the trap. AC 9 and 10 hit points invite the party to ignore it. The bite, at +1 to hit for 1d4, deserves to be ignored. The toxin does not. On a failed DC 12 Constitution save it drops a target unconscious for 1d4 rounds, and a seed with two or three Rot Flowers can incapacitate half the party while the real threat closes in. Prioritize killing it or keep distance. The flower's whole function is to make the group spend attention it would rather spend elsewhere.

The Doppelganger is a seed best framed with a timer, because its entire advantage is certainty. Change Shape swaps its attacks for transformation, so it fights by not fighting. Telepathy reads surface thoughts within near range, which turns your players' plans into its script. Give the party a reason to move and a clock that punishes hesitation. Repositioning constantly limits its telepathic reach and forces it to show its true form under pressure instead of on its own schedule. A party that trades certainty for speed survives. A party that waits to be sure gets played.

The Roper closes the lesson by charging a literal price for every wrong target. Its tendrils grab on a DC 15 Strength check and automatically hit the next round if not severed. The instinct is to free the grabbed ally first. That instinct gets a second party member pulled into range for the 2d8 bite. Cutting tendrils before freeing anyone is the only way to stop the group being reeled in like fish on a line. Focus fire on the tendrils is the whole fight.

The thread across all five is simple. The seed is the cost, not the creature. Poison, timing, toxin, telepathy, tendrils: each one punishes the party that reads the silhouette and ignores the mechanic.

**Running it:** Before you place any of these, write down the one price the party pays for ignoring its real threat, and make sure that price lands in round one.
